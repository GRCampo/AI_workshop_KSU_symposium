<h2>Connecting the model to your files</h2>

<div class="incident-body">
	<p>A connector gives the model a door into a service where you already have an account, for example a driver or a email. It walks through that door as you and it uses your credentials, so <strong>it can acess whatever your account contain.</strong></p>
	<p>Since it receives your permissions, if your account can open a file, the model can open it too, whether or not you were thinking about that file when you connected.</p>
</div>

<div class="incident-body">
	<p>Several companies offer this service. The mechanics are close enough that the vocabulary is almost interchangeable. What differs is who decides the connection is made, what the tool is allowed to do once connected, and what the vendor is willing to put in writing about it.</p>
	<p><strong>Checking the documentation from the company behind the model is still the best way to learn the details about that specific tool.</strong></p>
	<p>The table below summarizes some key questions about Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and Copilot connectors.</p>

<div class="featured-source">
	<div class="incident-body">
	<p><strong>So now the model can reach your files.</strong> and it is tempting to conclude that it <em>knows</em> them since your work is somehow in there.</p>
	</div>
</div>

<div class="incident-body">
	<p><strong>IT IS NOT!</strong></p>
	<p> A connector controls what can the model reach. It says nothing about what the model keeps.<strong> Everything the connector retrieves gets read once, used for one answer, and released.</strong></p>
</div>

<h2>So how does it actually answer?</h2>

<div class="incident-body">
	<p>The model processed an enormous amount of text during training, and what it kept was a set of weights (numerical parameters encoding the patterns in that text).</p>
	<p><strong>The model itself cannot reopen those texts, quote from them, or check anything against them.</strong></p>
	<p><strong>It can reach outside its weights:</strong> a web search, a file you upload, a connector. Without one of those, the answer comes from the weights alone — the model is not looking anything up, it is <a href="https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">generating what fits the shape of your question</a>.</p>
	<p>Those three routes differ in where the content comes from. They do not differ in what happens to it once it arrives.</p>
	<p>Everything you give the model arrives through one opening: the <strong>context window</strong>. The window is the whole of what the model knows about you, about this task, about this conversation.</p>
	<p><strong>And it is "repainted" with every question.</strong></p>
	<p></p>
</div>

	<img

<div class="incident-body">
	<p>Each message you send resends the entire conversation to be read again from the start, and the model does not remember having looked before. <a href="https://www.developerway.com/posts/how-ai-remembers-and-forgets-part1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The context is the only thing it holds about you</a>.</p>
	<p>Every message you send resends the whole conversation. The window does not accumulate memories; it accumulates <em>text</em>.</p>
</div>

<div class="incident-body">
	<p><strong>Quality degrades well before the limit</strong>, and the degradation is not evenly distributed: models handle the beginning and the end of a long context reasonably well and lose the middle.</p>
	<p></p>
</div>

<div class="incident-body">
	<p>The setup consists of a question and a stack of documents, only one of which contains the answer.</p>
	<p>The researchers moved this document through the stack (x-axis) without changing anything else. Adding more material does not just dilute the relevant part but moves it into the region the model handles worst.</p>
	<p><strong>The models tested are from 2023 but the patternstill happening.</strong></p>
</div>

<div class="incident-body">
	<p><a href="https://www.developerway.com/posts/how-ai-remembers-and-forgets-part1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">A developer ran this deliberately.</a> She put twenty of her own articles into a conversation and asked for a one-sentence summary of each, in a table.</p>
	<p>The conversation was using 65% of the context window the model had available but, across repeated runs, <strong>the model returned sixteen to nineteen summaries instead of twenty, dropping articles from the middle of the list, carried claims from one article into another, tt invented numbers, and in one run it produced a twenty-first row.</strong></p>
</div>


<div class="featured-source">
	<div class="incident-body">
	<p><strong>The failure is silent and it looks like an answer.</strong> A dropped paper does not come back as an error, and a fabricated number does not arrive flagged. If you are writing an introduction from that table, or a methods paragraph from a long analysis session, nothing in the output tells you which parts were reconstructed.</p>
	<p><strong>A long conversation is not a reproducible method.</strong> If an answer depends on what happened to be in the window at that moment, and the window is never the same twice, you cannot run it again and get it back.</p>
	<p><strong>Practical takeaways:</strong></p>
	<ul>
		<li><strong>One task per conversation.</strong> </li>
		<li><strong>Put critical instructions in a file, not in the chat.</strong></li>
		<li><strong>Repeat the constraint when it matters.</strong></li>
		<li><strong>Give the file, not the story about the file.</strong></li>
	</ul>
	</div>
</div>
