	<h2>About Me</h2>

	<div class="home-bio-stack">
		<img
			src="/AI_workshop_KSU_symposium/time_line_profi.png"

		<div class="home-ai-block">
			I graduated in <strong>Agronomic Engineering from ESALQ, University of São Paulo</strong>. I
			chose it six months before the admission exam and surprised almost everyone who knew me. I
			looked at where the demand was, looked at what I was actually good at (biology and math), and
			the two lined up. <em>I had no idea how much I would end up liking it!</em>

			During my second year, I took a genetics course. For someone with no previous experience in
			genetics, seeing Mendelian predictions from a Punnett square actually show up in real plants
			felt almost like magic. <em>That was when I realized this was what I wanted to do.</em>

			My first project was with maize doubled haploid lines: out to the field, crossing selected
			genotypes, selecting the progeny, back to the field again. It was also where I first had to
			analyze my own experimental data. The analyses were simple, though at the time they felt like
			rocket science, and learning R turned data analysis into a puzzle.

			<em>R became one of my favorite tools.</em> Python eventually joined the story, when I moved
			from traditional breeding into image-based phenotyping, but R will always have a special place
			in my heart.

			Then, during my <strong>Master's in Genetics and Plant Breeding</strong>, I moved into
			<em>plant–pathogen interactions and bioinformatics</em>: complex biological data, multiple
			computational tools, software versions, dependencies, and pipelines that occasionally seemed
			personally offended by my existence. More importantly, I had to learn how to turn complex data
			into information that could answer a biological question.

			And that is how I ended up here.

			Today I'm a PhD student building genomics and phenomics pipelines for wheat introgression
			lines, and most of the data I touch is not public.
		</div>
	</div>

	<div class="home-ai-block">
		I have been working with generative AI since the days when ChatGPT could not count the r's in "strawberry" while confidently explaining its answer like a Nobel Prize winner.

		Models are much better now, and in some ways much more intimidating. After using these tools for a while — making them fail, watching them succeed, and occasionally both in the same conversation — I learned that using AI well depends on understanding what a model can do, what it cannot, and where I still need to think for myself.

		<em>Curiosity</em> made me explore what these tools could actually do, and a healthy amount of <em>paranoia</em> made me ask what they were doing with my data.

		Repeated trial and error taught me where the useful shortcuts end and where scientific judgment has to take over.

		<p><strong>That is the perspective I bring to this workshop.</strong></p>
	</div>
